  \begin{abstract}
  Characterising the impact of human activities on wildlife is a key
  function of applied science. In the Antarctic Peninsula, the fishery
  for Antarctic krill has been operating for over half a century, and
  concerns that fishery-driven localised depletion of krill around
  pygoscelid penguin colonies could have deleterious effect on their
  performance and demographic trends have been raised. Watters et
  al.~(2020) investigated 30 years of penguin foraging and reproductive
  performance at two colonies in the South Shetland Islands, comparing
  these data with acoustic measurements of krill biomass and krill catch
  metrics at the gSSMU scale \citep{Watters2020}. Herein we critically
  assess the efficacy of this approach and its conceptual foundation,
  which has been cited as a basis for drawing conclusions as
  representing sound scientific advice in fisheries management
  organisations. We identify that several key assumptions in the Watters
  et al.~2020 study are contrary to the established scientific
  literature and not supported by the data provided by the authors
  themselves. By reformulating the modelling appraoch to address these
  issues, we find that the impact on penguin performance of fishing
  activity is substantially reduced. Instead, our analyses highlights
  the Oceanic Niño Index as the dominant driver of penguin performance.
  These findings identify that the conclusions in Watters et al.~(2020)
  should be treated with caution and not used to inform precautionary
  fisheries management advice.
  \end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Concerns over the potential impact of localised depletion of Antarctic
krill (\emph{Euphausia superba}) due to concentrated fishing effort,
particularly on krill-dependent predators during sensitive life stages
such as breeding, have been a longstanding topic of discussion within
the Antarctic science community. Recently, Watters et al.~(2020)
suggested that local harvesting rates may impact predator performance to
a degree comparable to that of adverse environmental conditions. Their
analyses matched various performance indices of krill-dependent penguins
with estimates of krill biomass, fishing pressure and broad scale
climate variability.

The penguin performance indices were collected at two sites (Cape
Shireff on Livingstone Island and Copacabana on King George Island,
South Shetland Islands; Figure 1) over the period 1982 to 2016. Krill
abundance data, representing the at-sea distributions of chinstrap,
gentoo and Adélie penguins, were collected during summer seasons (1996 -
2011) and winter seasons (2012, 2014 and 2015). Monthly krill catches
from Catch and Effort reports to CCAMLR, and climate variability was
represented by the Oceanic Niño Index (ONI). Using a hierarchical
analysis of variance approach, the authors estimated the variance in
penguin performance indices as a function of Local Krill Biomass (LKB),
Local Harvesting Rates (LHR; the ratio of krill catch to LKB) and the
(ONI.

Watters et al.~(2020) concluded that local harvesting levels of krill
adversely impact penguin performance, with a degree of impact comparable
to those of poor environmental conditions, and that these effects may be
synergistic when high local harvesting coincides with unfavorable
climate conditions. The conclusions of this paper, which as of this
writing has received 162 citations and has been by CCAMLR in support of
developing Marine Protected Area proposals, fisheries management
strategies as well as being used in the review process of the Marine
Stewardship Council fisheries certification process for the Antarctic
krill fishery.

Given the influence of this study on policy and certification
frameworks, it is essensial to ctitically assess its scientific
foundations. In this paper, we identify six critical issues that
undermine the validity of the conclusions presented by~Watters et
al.~(2020). For each issue, we provide contextual background and a
summary of the associated problem.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\emph{Issue \#1 Continuing spatial and
temporal mismatches predators and fishing
activity}}{Issue \#1 Continuing spatial and temporal mismatches predators and fishing activity}}\label{issue-1-continuing-spatial-and-temporal-mismatches-predators-and-fishing-activity}

A key goal for Watters et al.~(2020) was to highlight the consequences
of mismatched spatial and temporal scales between the management of the
Antarctic krill fishery and the ecological interactions between krill
extractions and krill-dependent predators. . To address this, the
authors created two strata composed of groups of Small-Scale Management
Units(gSSMU); gSSMU \#1 encompassing the Bransfield Strait (APBSE and
APBSW) and gSSMU \#2 covering areas north of the South Shetlands,
including Elephant Island (APDPE, APDPW and APEI) (Figure 1). These
strata were used to characterise both krill biomass and harvesting rates
considered ``local'' to the penguin colonies for which performance data
were available.

The reasoning behind scaling to gSSMU was linked to the foraging
behaviour of the breeding adult pygoscelid penguins. The authors cite
Hinke et al.~(2017) as the justification for the use of these strata.
However, pygoscelid penguins also exhibit staggered breeding phenology:
with Adélie penguins commencing first, followed by chinstrap penguins
then gentoo penguins (Black 2016). Adélie penguins are the first to
fledge their chicks and thus cease to be centrally foraging, typically
departing early February for their moulting grounds on the sea ice.
chinstrap penguins depart for a pre-moult foraging trip towards the
start of February and return to land in order to moult, before departing
again for their overwinter trip mid-March (Hinke et al.~2015); Hinke et
al.~2019). Conversely, gentoo penguins appear to remain near their
breeding colonies throughout the winter (Korczak-Abshire et al.~2021).

\emph{Despite these differences in seasonal movement and spatial
distribution, the model used by Watters et al.~(2020) assumes that
penguins occupy the entirety of their assigned gSSMU throughout the
year. This assumption introduces a spatial and temporal mismatch between
predator presence and fishing activity, potentially confounding the
interpretation of local harvesting impacts on penguin performance.}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Issue \#2: \emph{Unsupported Ecological
Assumptions}}{Issue \#2: Unsupported Ecological Assumptions}}\label{issue-2-unsupported-ecological-assumptions}

The conclusions presented by Watters at al.~(2020) are further
compromised by~unvalidated ecological assumptions, particularly the
claim that~krill biomass at spatial scales relevant to penguin foraging
varies systematically with the phase of the Southern Annular Mode (SAM).
While SAM is known to influence large-scale atmospheric circulation and
sea ice extent (REF), its~direct relationship to local krill biomass,
especially at the fine spatial scales relevant to penguin foraging,
has~not been empirically demonstrated.

The assumption that SAM-driven variability in krill biomass uniformly
affects penguin performance across broad regions~oversimplifies the
complex interplay of local oceanographic and biological processes. Krill
recruitment and aggregation~are influenced by multiple factors,
including~local prey availability, predation pressure, and fine-scale
hydrodynamic features, none of which are adequately captured by SAM
alone (REF). Without robust empirical support for this relationship, the
proposed mechanism linking climate variability, krill biomass, and
penguin performance remains~speculative.

Addressing this gap requires~finer-scale data~on krill distribution and
environmental drivers to validate, or refute, the ecological assumptions
underpinning the study's conclusions.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\emph{Issue \#3: Coding
errors}}{Issue \#3: Coding errors}}\label{issue-3-coding-errors}

A~coding error~further distorts the study's findings: the ``Worst Case''
scenario is described in the text as having a~neutral Oceanic Niño Index
(ONI)~but is incorrectly coded using a~warm ONI~parameter set
(Supplementary Material 1, lines 661--663). This misclassification
affects the interpretation of the model's outputs, particularly the
marginal probabilities associated with the ``Worst Case'' scenario.

Such structural coding errors results in misleading representations of
the modeled relationships between environmental conditions, krill
harvesting, and penguin performance. The presence of this error calls
into question the robustness of the study`s conclusions

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\emph{Issue \#4: Omission of Competing
Species}}{Issue \#4: Omission of Competing Species}}\label{issue-4-omission-of-competing-species}

Another significant limitation of~Watters et al.~(2020)~is its~failure
to account for competing krill-dependent predators. Recovering
populations of baleen whales ~(Baines et al.2021; Biuw et al.~2024;
Krafft et al., 2025) and~migratory male Antarctic fur seals (Lowther et
al.2020) forage in overlapping areas with penguins, particularly in key
regions such as the Bransfield Strait and Gerlache Strait
(e.g.~Hückstädt et al.~2012; Johannessen et al.~2022).

By ignoring the presence and consumption demands of these predators, the
study~overestimates the relative impact of the krill fishery~on penguin
performance. For example,~whales and seals~may exacerbate the effects of
fishing pressure by further depleting krill in shared foraging zones.
The absence of these ecological interactions in the model undermines the
completeness of the study`s conclusions.

A comprehensive assessment of fishery impacts on krill-dependent
predators~must incorporate the full suite of species competing for the
same resource. Without doing so, evaluations of competition dynamics and
their consequences for penguin populations remain incomplete and
potentially misleading.

The model used by Watters et al.~(2020) neglects the impact of
recovering populations of large-bodied krill eating predators that
overlap in time and space with penguins beyond a cursory discussion.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Issue \#5: \emph{Model
Bias}}{Issue \#5: Model Bias}}\label{issue-5-model-bias}

The analysis in~Watters et al.~(2020)~suffers from~two critical
methodological flaws~that undermine the reliability of its conclusions.
First, the model includes~both Local Krill Biomass (LKB) and Local
Harvest Rate (LHR)~as separate predictors, despite LHR being
mathematically derived from LKB (LHR = Catch / LKB). This~structural
dependency~introduces~multicollinearity, which compromises the ability
to disentangle the individual effects of krill availability and fishing
pressure on penguin performance.

For example, areas with low LKB will inherently yield higher LHR values
even under modest catch levels, thereby artificially inflating apparent
impact of fishing pressure.

\emph{PROBLEM STATEMENT - ULF}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\emph{Issue \#6: Over-Reliance on
Broad-Scale Climate
Indices}}{Issue \#6: Over-Reliance on Broad-Scale Climate Indices}}\label{issue-6-over-reliance-on-broad-scale-climate-indices}

A further limitation of~Watters et al.~(2020)~is its~reliance on
macroscale climate indices, such as the~Oceanic Niño Index (ONI)~and
the~Southern Annular Mode (SAM), to explain variability in krill
availability and penguin performance. While these indices are useful for
characterizing large-scale atmospheric and oceanographic patterns, their
application to ecological processes at fine spatial and temporal scales
relevant to penguin foraging is problematic.

The Amundsen Sea Low (ASL) is the dominant climate feature for the
western Antarctic Peninsula. The El Niño-Southern Oscillation (captured
in the ONI predictor) modulates the ASL, with El Niño (La Niña)
shallowing (deepening) its pressure, causing more northwesterly
(southeasterly) winds and upwelling (restricted influx) of Circumpolar
Deep Water onto the shelf. The SAM also influences the pressure of the
ASL, with the current trend of negative SAM constructively
(destructively) interfering with ASL when in phase with El Niño (La
Niña) events (e.g.~Clem et al.~2016).

The result is a set of above-surface climate conditions that drive
changes in water mass intrusion that is in turn dependent on
interactions between two climate processes as well as the geographical
orientation of the coastline involved, all of which have been shown to
impact the foraging trajectories and trip durations of Chinstrap
penguins (Andrew D. Lowther et al.~2018). The bathymetry of the
Antarctic Peninsula, which also influences the hydrographic conditions,
is complex particularly at scales that are important to
centrally-foraging predators such as penguins. The structuring of krill
aggregations in time and space in the WAP have also been linked to
mesoscale circulation processes (Santora et al.~2012), which are
unlikely to be uniformly affected by broad-scale climate indices due to
local variation in bathymetry and coastal orientation.

It is also worth noting that the study considers neither the impact of
climate on the terrestrial breeding grounds, such as chick mortality due
to rainfall-induced wetting (Chapman et al.~2011) nor does it unaccount
for measurement inaccuracies in foraging trip durations (Lowther et
al.~2015). While these indices provide useful insights into large-scale
atmospheric and oceanographic patterns, they~fail to capture the
finer-scale processes~that directly influence krill distribution and
predator-prey interactions. For example,~mesoscale oceanographic
features, such as local upwelling, sea ice dynamics, and bathymetric
influences, play a critical role in determining krill aggregation and
accessibility to penguins (REF). However, these processes operate at
spatial and temporal scales that are~not adequately reflected~by broad
indices like ONI or SAM (REF). As a result, the study's conclusions
regarding the impacts o climate impacts on penguin performance may
be~oversimplified or misrepresented, particularly in regions where local
conditions deviate from broader climate patterns. This overreliance on
coarse climate indices~limits the study's ability to accurately
assess~the true drivers of krill availability and, consequently, penguin
responses to environmental change. As such, the general structure of the
study does not appear to be appropriate for answering the research
questions raised.

\emph{The model used by Watters et al.~(2020) relies exclusively on
ocean-scale climate indices as environmental predictors of LKB and
penguin performance.}

Herein, we conduct analyses and discuss these to demonstrate the
inappropriate or unsupported conclusions related to Issues \#1 to \#3
and the potential impact that Issue \#4 raises in understanding the
drivers of penguin performance. We then discuss Issues \#5 and \#6
qualitatively, with no associated numerical analyses.

\section{Materials and Methods}\label{materials-and-methods}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\emph{Issue \#1: Continuing spatial and
temporal
mismatches}}{Issue \#1: Continuing spatial and temporal mismatches}}\label{issue-1-continuing-spatial-and-temporal-mismatches}

To assess the spatial overlap between krill-dependant predators and
fishing activity, we utilzed Argos-CLS PTT telemetry data provided by
the supporting studies referenced in Watters et al.~(2020). These data
were used to characterise the actual at-sea habitat use of breeding
adult pygoscelid penguins, contextualized by species-specific breeding
stages. Additional sources, including Warwick-Evans et al.~(2018) and
Lowther et al.~(2018), were consulted to further quantify foraging
behaviour in the study region.

For each species, we refrain from undertaking extensive state-space
modelling of location errors and merely exclude locations with a ``Z''
error class, accepting the remaining locations had varying degrees of
uncertainty around them. We then calculated the 99\% Minimum Convex
Polygon (home range) using the R package adehabitatHR and calculate
their associated areas in , with the resulting MCP likely being larger
than the area actually exploited, given the remaining inaccuracy in the
underlying locations used.We then scale the gSSMU LKB to the SSMU that
the summer tracking data indicate penguins occupied. To do this, we
calculate the area () of the SSMU for which the predator occupies and
the gSSMU to which it is assigned, then create a scaling ratio. For
example, we scale LKB for Cape Shireff chinstrap penguins solely to
ADPDW (Figure 1) by multiplying the gSSMU LKB by the areal ratio of
ADPDW/gSSMU \#2. We then select the corresponding SSMU catch values
provided in Watters et al.~(2020) (Supplementary Info) to estimate
SSMU-scale LHR. We also caution that while considering the gSSMU scale
of harvesting as inappropriate for ``local'' effects, even the
SSMU-scale catch levels likely do not accurately reflect pressures at
the fine spatial scales relevant to breeding penguins

Watters et al.~(2020) assigned LKB/LHR values in March into the
``summer'' period, thus effectively incorporating fishing pressure into
the summer breeding season in the context of penguin performance
metrics. However, fishing effort over the 30-year study period was not
temporally uniform. In the last decade, catch rates have shown a
nonlinear increase beginning in mid March, with the three years
exhibiting rapid increases from early March (Figure 4). Given this
variability, we conducted models runs that classified March as either
part of the summer or winter season, based on the breeding phenology of
Adélie and chinstrap penguins, both of which typically cease
central-place foraging or migrate out of the area by March (Hinke et
al., 2015, HINKE REFS GALORE, INCLUDING 2025 Scientific Reports).

Thus we reformulate the underlying assumptions above into a new model
construct, in which performance indices from gentoo, chinstrap and
Adélie penguins during the summer are included, but Adélie and chinstrap
penguins cease to be centrally foraging species after breeding and
migrate out of the area. The performance indices are matched in space
and time but using SSMU level estimates of LKB and LHR. We re-run the
model twice 1) using gappy survey data and 2) including imputed values
for LKB in years where survey data are missing. We further consider two
alternatives for considering March, either in a) summer or b) winter on
each model run.

We present the outputs both in the same boxplot format as Figure 2 in
the original manuscript, and as individual cases grouped and
colour-coded as ONI ``warm'' ( 0.5; red), ONI ``neutral'' (-0.5
\textless{} ONI \textgreater{} +0.5; white) and ONI ``cold''
(\textless{} -0.5; blue). We also recreate the original marginal
probabilities in Table 1 of Watters et al.~(2020), and two additional
tables in the same format with the probabilities extracted from our
reformulated model, the difference between the latter two tables
reflecting whether March is in summer or winter.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\emph{Issue \#3: Model Coding
Error}}{Issue \#3: Model Coding Error}}\label{issue-3-model-coding-error}

We also note an additional coding error that may influence how the
original, unmodified results are interpreted. In summarising the model
outputs into boxplots, the text in the paper seemingly classifies the
``Worst Case'' with ``neutral'' ONI ( oC \textless{} ONI \textless{} 0.5
oC; LKB \textgreater{} 1 Mt; and LHR 0.1) and the code relating to
developing the original manuscript Figure 2 uses Parameter set 36 from
the output dataframe, which actually reflects a ``warm'' ONI component
(\textgreater{} 0.5 oC; LKB \textgreater{} 1 Mt; and LHR 0.1). Yet the
discussion in Watters, Hinke, and Reiss (2020) also suggests that the
likelihood of their ``Worst Case'' includes future warming (see Figure 3
below)

We agree that any ``Worst Case'' should reflect ENSO conditions into the
future under a warming climate. However, climate change is likely to
increase ENSO in amplitude - both El Niño (ONI ``warm'') and La Niña
(ONI ``cold'') (Capotondi et al.~2015). How this increasing amplitude
can be integrated appropriately into the presented modelling framework
to match with long-term predicted mean performance of predators has not
been explored yet. As such, and for the sake of comparison with the
original study, we maintain the authors designation of ONI ``neutral''
when rendering the ``Worst Case'' boxplots, though caution that this is
unlikely to be a realistic assumption

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\emph{Issue \#4: Omission of Competing
Species}}{Issue \#4: Omission of Competing Species}}\label{issue-4-omission-of-competing-species-1}

In order to provide numerical context on the potential role baleen
whales and Antarctic fur seals may play in regulating penguin
performance, we conduct two analyses. Firstly, we use published
estimates of the time-evolving abundance and distribution of humpback
whales (Megaptera novaeangliae) throughout the Bransfield Strait
(Johannessen et al.~2022) to estimate the number of whales present in
the SSMU occupied by penguins from Copacabana. We then use conservative
per-capita consumption estimates (REF) to derive monthly krill
consumption rates and compare these to the SSMU krill catch provided in
Watters et al.~(2020). Secondly, we use per-capita krill consumption
rates for adult male Antarctic fur seals which are known to migrate into
the region from as early as January until the beginning of the following
summer breeding season (Lowther et al.~2020). The catch limit in the
management unit in which the Watters et al.~(2020) is conducted was
155,000tonnes/year, and we back-calculate the number of Antarctic fur
seals that would need to be present to at least equal the krill removal
rate of the fishery.

All analyses are conducted in the R statistical software environment (R
4.5.1), using the original data provided in Watters et al.~(2020) or in
the published articles cited as supporting evidence. To faciliate
reproducibility, the Supplementary Material from the original Watters et
al.~(2020) article and the Supplementary Material from all necessary
supporting articles are hosted in Zenodo repositories (record ID 5040114
and record ID 17347172). A dedicated R script allowing for these data
and scripts to be downloaded directly from the repositories is provided
in Appendix I. Fully annotated R code, highlighting the modifications
and justifications, as well as the code needed to fully reproduce the
figures and tables presented here, are also included in the Zenodo
repositories.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\emph{Issue \#1: Continuing spatial and
temporal
mismatches}}{Issue \#1: Continuing spatial and temporal mismatches}}\label{issue-1-continuing-spatial-and-temporal-mismatches-1}

Comparing the original model outputs with ours, relative to the ``Best
Case'', the probability of negative impacts to penguins due to high LHR
dropped precipitously from 93\% to 37\% (Table 3). In other words, when
considering the migration of penguins in accordance with their known
ecology (Figure 2), the relative probability of negative impact of LHR
drops from a near-certainty to 1-in-3 (Table 2 and 3). At this point, we
clarify that the probability of negative impact is not a measure of the
scale of the expected impact (i.e.~a measure of population decline), but
a measure of whether any negative impact is likely to occur. Given the
temporal separation between fishing and penguin breeding over the
preceding decade, our results are unsurprising.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

Our reanalysis of~Watters et al.~(2020)~reveals that the study's
conclusions are based on~assumptions that lack empirical support. By
addressing~spatial and temporal mismatches,~unvalidated ecological
assumptions, and~methodological biases, we demonstrate that
the~perceived impacts of krill fishing on penguin performance are
significantly overestimated. Specifically, rescaling spatial units
from~gSSMUs to SSMUs~and accounting for~species-specific migration
patterns~reduced the estimated probability of negative impacts from high
Local Harvest Rates (LHR)~from 93\% to 37\%. Additionally, our findings
highlight that~broad-scale climate indices (ONI, SAM)~are less
influential than~local oceanographic processes~in determining krill
availability and penguin performance.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\emph{Issue \#1: Spatial and temporal
mismatches undermine study
conclusions}}{Issue \#1: Spatial and temporal mismatches undermine study conclusions}}\label{issue-1-spatial-and-temporal-mismatches-undermine-study-conclusions}

The~spatial scales~used in Watters et al.~(2020) exceed the actual
foraging ranges of penguins by a factor of~4 to 15 For example, Adélie
penguins forage within~1,139 nm², yet the study used~gSSMUs of
15,500--20,600 nm²~to define ``local'' krill biomass and harvest rates.
This mismatch leads to~overestimated localized depletion effects, as the
broad spatial units fail to capture the fine-scale interactions between
penguins and their prey.

Similarly, the study's~temporal assumptions~incorrectly associate winter
fishing pressure with penguin performance, despite Adélie and chinstrap
penguins~migrating away from breeding colonies by late February. By
excluding these species from winter analyses, we show that the~original
model's conclusions about seasonal fishing impacts are misassigned.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\emph{Issue \#2: Unvalidated Ecological
Assumptions}}{Issue \#2: Unvalidated Ecological Assumptions}}\label{issue-2-unvalidated-ecological-assumptions-1}

Watters et al.~(2020) assumes a direct relationship between~krill
biomass and the phase of the Southern Annular Mode (SAM), yet our
statistical analyses reveal~no significant correlation~between SAM
phases and Local Krill Biomass (LKB). Similarly, the assumption that~low
LKB (\textless1 Mt) corresponds to smaller krill~is not supported by
empirical evidence. These oversimplifications obscure the complex
ecological processes that govern k krill-penguin dynamics.

Additionally, the study overlooks the role of krill life-stage
dynamics~in shaping prey availability and energetic value for predators.
Krill populations consist of~multiple size classes and age cohorts, each
exhibiting distinct spatial distributions, seasonal behaviors, and
nutritional profiles (REF). For instance, small juvenile krill, often
dominate during recruitment pulses, are~less energetically valuable~to
penguins than larger, mature individuals (REF). Seasonal and spatial
variability in krill size structure driven by recruitment events and
environmental conditions, can significantly influence predator foraging
success and reproductive outcomes (REF), yet these dynamics are~not
explicitly considered~in Watters et al.~(2020).

By treating krill biomass as a~homogeneous metric, the authors fail to
account for critical variations in krill quality and availability that
may drive penguin foraging success or failure. Addressing this gap
requires~life-stage-specific data~on krill distribution and predator
preferences to refine assessments of how fishing and environmental
changes affect penguin populations.

Together, these oversights highlight the need for~finer-scale ecological
data~to validate, or refute, the mechanisms proposed by Watters et al.
(2020).

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\emph{Issue \#3: Model coding
error}}{Issue \#3: Model coding error}}\label{issue-3-model-coding-error-1}

A structural coding error in Watters et al.~(2020) further compromises
the validity of the study's conclusions. The ``Worst Case'' scenario is
described in the text as having a neutral Oceanic Niño Index (ONI) but
was incorrectly coded using parameters for a warm ONI. This
misclassification inflates the perceived severity of neutral ONI
conditions, leading to misinterpretation of the model's outputs,
particularly the marginal probabilities associated with the ``Worst
Case'' scenario. The coding error reduces the probability of negative
impacts from high LHR from 93\% to 37\% (Table 3). This adjustment
underscores the dependency of perceived fishing pressure effects on
krill abundance: low LKB amplifies the apparent impact of LHR, while
high LKB mitigates it. Furthermore, the revised model clarifies that ONI
excerts a stronger influence on penguin performance than LHR when
structural biases are removed, challenging the original study`s emphasis
on harvesting effects.

\subsection{}\label{section}

Issue \#4: \emph{Omission of competing krill predators}

A major limitation of Watters et al.~(2020) is the omission of
krill-dependent predators that compete with penguins for prey resources.
Recovering baleen whale populations~and~migratory male Antarctic fur
seals exert substantial predation pressure on krill, particularly in
regions that overlap with penguin breeding and foraging activity. Using
available abundance, distribution and consumption estimates for
competing species, we show that humpback whales consume over five times
the amount of krill than taken by the fishery per month. We show that
the number of male Antarctic fur seals required to match the LHR of the
fishery for each year of the study ranges from as few as zero to as many
as 72,000 seals; given the number of seals that are recorded at only two
sites in the South Orkney Islands (32,000 individuals) and their
movements into the Antarctic Peninsula are well documented (Lowther et
al.~2020), there are likely to be significant competitive interactions
between migrating seals and breeding penguins. The consumption by whales
also overlaps temporally with penguin breeding, unlike the fishery which
since 2010 has shifted to a winter fishery (REF). Intuitively then,
ignoring the vast amount more krill removed by whales at the same time
as breeding penguins are foraging is likely to be greatly overestimating
the relative impact of fisheries removal on penguin performance. Future
assessments of krill fishery must incorporate~multi-species
interactions~to accurately evaluate the cumulative pressures on
krill-dependent predators.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\emph{Issue \#5: Model
Bias}}{Issue \#5: Model Bias}}\label{issue-5-model-bias-1}

The presence of~structural model bias~arising from the confounding
relationship between covariates (LHR and LKB; LHR=Catch/LKB) was not
explicitly explored in this study. This dependency, however, masks the
isolated effects of krill availability (LKB) and fishing pressure
(Catch) on penguin performance, potentially leading to misleading
conclusions about the relative importance of these factors. This
structural confounding in the model results in~artificial inflation of
fishing impacts~in areas with low LKB, where even modest catches yield
high LHR values, thereby exaggerating the perceived impact of fishing
pressure on penguin performance. Conversely, in areas with high LKB,
substantial catches may be masked because the same absolute catch
results in a lower LHR, underestimating the true effects of fishing.
This structural bias undermines the study's ability to disentangle the
true ecological drivers of penguin performance, necessitating a
reevaluation of the model's specifications and assumptions. These
findings underscore the necessity for methodological rigor in ecological
modeling. Future studies should avoid multicollinearity by using Catch
and LKB as separate predictors instead of LHR to isolate the effects of
fishing pressure and krill availability. Additionally, validating coding
and assumptions is crucial to ensure that scenario classifications align
with described conditions and prevent misinterpretation. Finally,
reevaluating model specifications to account for structural biases will
enable more accurate assessments of the impacts of fishing on predator
performance. 6 Over-reliance on broad-scale climate indices Watters et
al.~(2020) relies on~macroscale climate indices (ONI, SAM)~to explain
variability in krill availability, yet these indices fail to
capture~finer-scale oceanographic processes~(e.g., local upwelling, sea
ice dynamics) that directly influence krill aggregation. Our reanalysis
shows that~local environmental predictors~(e.g., sea ice concentration,
chlorophyll-a) explain more variance in penguin performance than ONI or
SAM. This over-reliance on broad-scale indices~limits the study's
ability to accurately assess~the true drivers of krill availability and
penguin responses to environmental change.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\emph{Implications for fisheries
management}}{Implications for fisheries management}}\label{implications-for-fisheries-management}

The~real-world implications~of Watters et al.~(2020) are substantial, as
its conclusions have influenced~CCAMLR's Marine Protected Area
proposals~and~fisheries certification processes. However, our reanalyses
suggest that key~methodological limitations, including spatial and
temporal mismatches, unvalidated ecological assumptions, and the
omission of competiting predators, compromise the study`s validity as
basis for management advice. We recommend that future assessments: 1.
Use~spatial scales aligned with penguin foraging ranges~(such as SSMUs
rather than gSSMUs to beter reflect ecological interactions). 2. Account
for~species-specific migration and seasonal variability in fishing
effort, particularly in relation to breeding phenology. 3.
Incorporate~competing predators~and~local environmental drivers,
including mesoscale oceanographic processes and krill life-stage
dynamics. By addressing these issues, managers can develop~more accurate
and evidence-based strategies~for balancing krill harvesting with the
conservation of dependant predator populations.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\emph{Broader concerns and future
directions}}{Broader concerns and future directions}}\label{broader-concerns-and-future-directions}

While Watters et al.~(2020) raises important questions regarding the
ecological impacts of krill fishery, its analytical framework,~ focused
solely on fishing pressure and climate indices, is~insufficient~for
disentangling the~relative contributions of multiple stressors affecting
krill-dependent predators. Over~20 years ago, CCAMLR acknowledged that
its~CEMP monitoring program~was unable to distinguish between the
effects of fishing and climate variability (REF), yet no substantive
modifications have been implemented to address this limitation. Moving
forward,~integrated monitoring programs~are needed to capture the
complexity of predator-prey interactions in the Antarctic ecosystem.
~Such programs should incorporate data on interspecific competition,
local oceanographic conditions, and fine-scale predator behavior. These
elements are essential for developing a more accurate understanding of
the cumulative pressures on krill-dependent species and for informing
sustainable fisheries management under changing environmental
conditions.

\subsection{}\label{section-1}

\emph{Conclusion}

Our reanalysis of Watters et al.~(2020) demonstrates thet the study`s
conclusions are substantially influenced by methodlogogical limitations,
including spatial and spatial/temporal mismatches, unsupported
ecological assumptions, omission of competing krill predators and
structural biases in model design.. While~climate variability
(ONI)~remains a dominant driver of penguin performance, the~impacts of
krill fishing are considerable less severe~when evaluated
at~ecologically relevant spatial and temporal scales.

To ensure that scientific advice provided to CCAMLR is both objective
and robust, future assessments must adopt~finer resolution spatial
frameworks aligned with predator foraging ranges, account for
species-specific seasonal dynamics, and incorporate~multi-species
interactions and local environmental drivers. Only through such
integrative approaches can we accurately assess the cumulative impacts
of fishing and environmental change~on Antarctic krill-dependent
predators in the Southern ocean ecosystem.\newpage
