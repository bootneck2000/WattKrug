  \begin{abstract}
  Assessing the impacts of human activities on wildlife is a critical
  function of applied ecology. In the Antarctic Peninsula, the krill
  fishery fhas been operated for over half a century, raising concerns
  about localised depletion of krill around pygoscelid penguin colonies
  and its potential impacts on penguin populations. Watters et
  al.~(2020) concluded that krill harvesting adversely affects penguin
  performance, comparable to the impacts of poor environmental
  conditions, based on 30 years data from two South Shetland Islands,
  Antartic Peninsula. However, their study relies on appropriate spatial
  and temporal scales, unvalidated ecological assumptions, and
  methodological biases. Herein, we reassess the original model by
  rescaling spatial units from group Small-Scale Management Units
  (gSSMUs) to ecologically relevant small-scale management units,
  excluding non-breeding penguins from winter analyses to reflect their
  known migration patterns, and correcting a coding error that
  misclassified the ``Worst Case'' climate scenario. Our analysis
  reveals that the probability of negative impacts from high Local
  Harves Rates (LHR) drops from 93\% to 27\% after these adjustments.
  Instead, climate variability, as represented by the Oceanic Niño Index
  (ONI), emerges as the dominant driver of penguin performance. These
  findings challange the conclusions of Watters et al.~(2020) and
  emphasise the need for more rigorous, ecologically realistic models to
  inform krill fisheries management in the Antarctic Peninsula.
  \end{abstract}
  
 \end{frontmatter}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Concerns about the potential impact of localised depletion of Antarctic
krill (Euphausia superba; hereafter, krill) due to concentrated fishing
effort, particularly on krill-dependent predators such as penguins
during breeding, have long been debated within the Antarctic science
community. Recently, Watters et al.~(2020) suggested that local
harvesting rates may impact penguin performance to a degree comparable
to that of adverse environmental conditions. Their analyses matched
various performance indices of krill-dependent penguins with estimates
of krill biomass, local fishing pressure and broad scale climate
variability.

The penguin breeding parameters were collected at two sites (Cape
Shirreff on Livingstone Island and Copacabana on King George Island,
South Shetland Islands; Figure 1) over the period 1982 to 2016. Krill
abundance data, overlapping the at-sea distributions of Chinstrap
(Pygoscelis antarcticus), Gentoo (P. papua) and Adélie penguins (P.
adeliae), were collected during summer seasons (1996 - 2011) and winter
seasons (2012, 2014 and 2015). Catch data from the krill fishery were
obtained from the Commission for the Conservation of Antarctic Marine
Living Resources (CCAMLR, www.ccamlr.org) for the entire period. Climate
variability was represented by the Oceanic Niño Index (ONI). Using a
hierarchical analysis of variance approach, the authors estimated the
variance in penguin performance indices as a function of Local Krill
Biomass (LKB), Local Harvesting Rate (LHR; the ratio of krill catch to
LKB) and the ONI. Missing summer LKB data was imputed using a presumed
correlation with the Southern Annular Mode (SAM).

Watters et al.(2020), concluded that local harvesting levels of krill
adversely impact penguin performance, with effects comparable in
magnitude to those of poor environmental conditions. Moreover, they
found that these effects may act synergistically when high local
harvesting rates coincide with unfavorable climate conditions. The
paper, which has received significant attention and citations, has
influenced CCAMLR's work on Marine Protected Area proposals and krill
fisheries management strategies.

Given the influence of this study on policy and certification
frameworks, it is essential to critically assess its scientific
foundations. In this paper, we identify three critical issues that
undermine the validity of the conclusions presented by~Watters et
al.~(2020). For each issue, we provide a summary of the associated
issues, while detailed contextual information on each issue is presented
in Supplementary Materials \#1. Issue \#1: Missing data and Unsupported
Ecological Assumptions

Despite issues on survey coverage, data used in the analysis was
unfiltered, potentially yielding unreliable or inaccurate estimates of
LKB. Likewise, the lack of tested correlation between krill biomass and
SAM could also result in unreliable or inaccurate estimates of LKB.
Finally, the assignment of `best' and `worst' case scenarios based on a
given level of krill biomass (in this case using 1Mt as the threshold),
was not tested throughout the paper. These factors introduced
significant inaccuracies in the estimation of LHR, making the
interpretation of local harvesting impacts on penguin performance
unreliable.

Issue \#2: Continuing spatial and temporal mismatches between predators
and fishing activity

The model used by Watters et al.~(2020) assumes that penguins occupy the
entirety of their assigned gSSMU throughout the year. This assumption
introduces a spatial and temporal mismatch between predator presence and
fishing activity, potentially confounding the interpretation of local
harvesting impacts on penguin performance. Issue \#3: Omission of
Competing Species

The model used by Watters et al.~(2020) neglects the impact of
recovering populations of large krill eating predators that overlap in
time and space with penguins, beyond a cursory discussion.

In the following, we present quantitative analyses addressing Issues \#1
to \#3, demonstrating the limitations and inaccuracies in the study`s
conclusions and highlighting their implications for understanding the
drivers of penguin performance and the robustness of the findings
presented by Watters et al.~(2020).

\section{Materials and Methods}\label{materials-and-methods}

\subsubsection{Issue \#1: Missing data and Unvalidated Ecological
Assumptions}\label{issue-1-missing-data-and-unvalidated-ecological-assumptions}

We filtered local krill biomass (LKB) estimates to remove surveys with
low spatial coverage. Watters et al.~(2020) proposed excluding surveys
with transect coverage of 80 nm or less, but did not apply this
criterion (Supplementary Material S1, lines 224--226). Rather than
applying a fixed threshold, we examined the frequency distribution of
acoustic transect coverage separately for gSSMUs \#1 and \#2 and
excluded surveys falling at or below the lower 10th percentile.

We evaluated the assumptions used by Watters et al.~(2020) to impute
summer LKB in years without acoustic surveys. We further examined the
relationship used to impute missing LKB values and whether the
partitioning of LKB relative to SAM phase and a 1 Mt threshold was
consistent with observed patterns. Their approach classified LKB into
two categories, ≤1 Mt and \textgreater1 Mt, and assigned missing values
based solely on the sign of the SAM index. Under this framework, LKB ≤1
Mt defined the ``best case,'' while LKB \textgreater1 Mt defined the
``worst case.'' however the rationale for both the SAM-based assignment
and the 1 Mt threshold was not provided. We tested these assumptions
empirically for each gSSMU by comparing the median observed LKB between
negative and positive SAM years using a Kruskal-Wallis H test. We then
evaluated whether 1 Mt represented an appropriate threshold separating
SAM phases. Following equation (2) in Watters et al.~(2020), we
estimated the mean and standard deviation of ln(LKB) separately for
negative and positive SAM years within each gSSMU. Differences between
SAM phases were tested using a one-way permutation test implemented in
the R package coin.

We also evaluated the statistical properties of the LKB imputation
procedure itself. We first assessed whether ln(LKB) derived from survey
observations followed a suitable parametric distribution. We then
generated imputed ln(LKB) values using equations (1) and (2) of Watters
et al.~(2020) and compared the resulting distributions with the
empirical distribution of observed data. A log-normal distribution was
fitted to survey-based LKB estimates, and normality of ln(LKB) was
assessed using the Shapiro--Wilk test. Using the same parameterization,
we generated 100,000 imputed ln(LKB) values, constrained by lower and
upper bounds of 10,000 t and 1 × 10⁹ t, respectively, as defined in
Supplementary Material S1. Normality of the imputed ln(LKB) distribution
was then assessed using the same approach.

Finally, local harvest rate (LHR) was calculated using observed catch
data provided by CCAMLR and observed LKB for each year and gSSMU.
Imputed LHR values were calculated using observed catch data and the
median imputed LKB for negative and positive SAM years.

\subsubsection{Issue \#2: Continuing spatial and temporal
mismatches}\label{issue-2-continuing-spatial-and-temporal-mismatches}

To assess the spatial overlap between krill-dependant predators and
fishing activity, we utilized Argos-CLS PTT telemetry data used by
Watters et al.~(2020) from the supporting studies referenced therein.
These data were used to characterise the actual at-sea habitat use of
breeding adult pygoscelid penguins, contextualized by species-specific
breeding stages, relative to the gSSMU structure used.

For each species, we refrain from undertaking extensive state-space
modelling of location errors and merely exclude locations with a ``Z''
error class, accepting the remaining locations had varying degrees of
uncertainty around them. We then calculated the 90\% kernel Utilisation
Distribution (UD) to represent home range using the R package
adehabitatHR and calculated their associated areas, with the resulting
UD likely being larger than the area actually exploited given the
remaining inaccuracy in the underlying locations used. We then scale the
gSSMU LKB to the SSMU that the summer tracking data indicated penguins
occupied. To do this, we calculate the area of the SSMU exploited by the
predator and the gSSMU to which it is assigned, then created a scaling
ratio. For example, we scaled LKB for Cape Shirreff Chinstrap penguins
solely to APDPW (Figure 1) by multiplying the gSSMU \#2 LKB by the areal
ratio of APDPW / gSSMU \#2. We then select the corresponding SSMU catch
values provided in Watters et al.~(2020) (Supplementary Info) to
estimate SSMU-scale LHR. We also caution that while considering the
gSSMU scale of harvesting as inappropriate for ``local'' effects, even
the SSMU-scale catch levels likely do not accurately reflect pressures
at the fine spatial scales relevant to breeding penguins.

Watters et al.~(2020) assigned LKB/LHR values in March into the
``summer'' period. Given that contemporary fishing patterns, this
effectively incorporates fishing pressure into the summer breeding
season in the context of penguin performance metrics. Yet the `summer'
predator indices used were estimated during field work that was
finalized before March each season, thus not incorporating fishing
effect during March. Importantly, the breeding phenology of pygoscelid
penguins in this area over the period of this study strongly suggests
that breeding is finished in February1--4. In addition, fishing effort
over the 30-year study period was not temporally uniform with catch
rates over the last decade showing a nonlinear increase beginning in mid
March5. Given this variability, we conducted models runs that classified
March as either part of the summer or winter season.

We therefore reformulated the model construct by incorporating
performance indices for gentoo, chinstrap, and Adélie penguins during
the summer, while treating Adélie and chinstrap penguins as non-resident
species post-breeding --- ceasing central-place foraging activity and
migrating out of the study area. Following exclusion of surveys with low
spatial coverage, we run models in which (i) penguin distributions in
summer and winter are constrained to SSMUs supported by telemetry data
provided in the original study, and (ii) catch data are allocated from
March onward through the winter period.

We present the outputs both in the same boxplot format as Figure 2 in
the original manuscript, and as individual cases grouped and
colour-coded as ONI ``warm'' (0.5; red), ONI ``neutral'' (-0.5
\textless{} ONI \textgreater{} +0.5; white) and ONI ``cold''
(\textless{} -0.5; blue). We also recreate the original marginal
probabilities in Table 1 of Watters et al.~(2020), and two additional
tables in the same format with the probabilities extracted from our
reformulated model, the difference between the latter two tables
reflecting whether March is in summer or winter.

\subsubsection{Issue \#3: Omission of competing
species}\label{issue-3-omission-of-competing-species}

In order to provide numerical context on the potential role baleen
whales may play in regulating penguin performance, we conduct two
analyses. Firstly, we use published data on the time-evolving abundance
and distribution of humpback whales (Megaptera novaeangliae) throughout
the Bransfield Strait6 to estimate the mean number of whales present in
gSSMU \#1 (occupied by penguins from Copacabana). We then use
conservative per-capita consumption estimates (632kg/day) 7 to derive a
90-day summer season krill consumption estimate at the gSSMU scale.
Finally we compare humpback whale consumption estimates to the 32-years
of krill catches provided in Watters et al.~(2020) at the same spatial
scale, cropped to the summer season (November through February
inclusive).

All analyses were conducted in the R statistical software environment (R
4.5.1), using the original data provided in Watters et al.~(2020) or in
the published articles cited as supporting evidence. To facilitate
reproducibility, the Supplementary Material from the original Watters et
al.~(2020) article and the Supplementary Material from all necessary
supporting articles are hosted in Zenodo repositories (record ID 5040114
and record ID 17347172). A dedicated R script allowing for these data
and scripts to be downloaded directly from the repositories is provided
in Appendix I. Fully annotated R code, highlighting the modifications
and justifications, as well as the code needed to fully reproduce the
figures and tables presented here, are also included in the Zenodo
repositories.

\section{Results}\label{results}

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\emph{Issue \#1: Missing data and
Unvalidated Ecological
Assumptions}}{Issue \#1: Missing data and Unvalidated Ecological Assumptions}}\label{issue-1-missing-data-and-unvalidated-ecological-assumptions-1}

Survey coverage varied across years and survey legs, resulting in the
exclusion of surveys with transect coverage at or below the 10th
percentile removing four survey legs in gSSMU 1 (≤10 nm; 1996, 1999,
2010, 2012) and three survey legs in gSSMU 2 (≤200 nm; 2010, 2011, 2012)
(Supplementary Material, Figure S1). All subsequent analyses were based
on the filtered survey dataset.

No significant differences in ln(LKB) were detected between negative and
positive SAM years for either gSSMU (Table 1, Figure 2). Median LKB
differed between gSSMUs, with consistently higher values in gSSMU \#2
than in gSSMU \#1 across both SAM phases. Mean ln(LKB) differed between
SAM phases only for gSSMU \#2 (p-value\_2 = 0.008), with higher values
observed during positive SAM years. Median LKB values for gSSMU 2
exceeded 1 Mt in all years with survey data, regardless of SAM phase
(Figure 2). Consequently, by applying an arbitrary 1 Mt threshold to
gSSMU \#2 LKB data would wrongly classify all imputed-LKB years as
``worst case'' scenario.

Observed survey-based LKB distributions for all gSSMU--SAM combinations
were well described by a log-normal distribution (Supplementary
Material, Table S1; Figure S2). In contrast, ln(LKB) values generated
through the imputation procedure of Watters et al.~(2020) were
concentrated toward the upper bound of the imposed limits, with mean
ln(LKB) values of 17.5--17.7 (approximately 44 Mt), and did not conform
to a log-normal distribution (Supplementary Material, Table S2; Figure
S3). As above, this bias would wrongly classify all LKB-imputed years,
for both gSSMUs, as ``worst case'' scenario.

Local harvest rate (LHR) estimates were recalculated using observed LKB
values and imputed LKB values (median LKB, as presented in Table 1)
(Figure 3). In gSSMU \#1, LHR was \textless0.01 in most years with
survey data, with intermediate values observed during summer 2010 (LHR =
0.0165) and winter 2014 (LHR = 0.012); LHR exceeded 0.10 only in winter
2015. When imputed summer values were included, LHR ranged from 0.034 to
0.067 during summers 2013--2015. In gSSMU \#2, all years with
survey-based LKB had LHR \textless0.01, while intermediate LHR values
occurred only in years with imputed LKB (1987, 1988, 1989, 1992) (Figure
3).

Penguin performance was reanalysed to assess the effects of excluding
low-coverage surveys and revising imputed summer LKB values using
estimates in Table 1 (Supplementary Material, Section C). Penguin
performance was most strongly associated with intermediate ONI and
intermediate LHR conditions. The nominal ``worst case'' scenario no
longer produced the lowest expected performance and instead lay close to
the long-term mean. Notably, scenarios with high harvest rates (LHR ≥
0.10) exhibited median performance comparable to the ``best case''
scenario (Figures S5 and S7).

Because imputing missing LKB values produced no substantive change in
posterior predictive distributions of penguin performance, and analyses
based solely on observed data yielded slightly lower (more conservative)
estimates, all subsequent results are reported without imputing missing
LKB values.

\subsubsection{Issue \#2: Continuing spatial and temporal
mismatches}\label{issue-2-continuing-spatial-and-temporal-mismatches-1}

Foraging distribution of adult Gentoo, Chinstrap and Adélie penguins
during breeding are more restricted than Watters et al.~(2020) assumed.
Breeding penguins at Copacabana utilize mainly SSMU APBSE (south of King
George Island), occupying approximately 15\% of gSSMU \#1 while
conspecificis from Cape Shirreff exploited less than 20\% of gSSMU \#2,
primarily in SSMUs BSW and APDPW, respectively (north of Livingstone
Island) (Figure 4, Supplementary Table SX).

Adult foraging distribution before the breeding season, which presumably
determines ``winter'' performance, is broader than during summer (Figure
5). Chinstrap Penguins disperse along the southern Antarctic circumpolar
current front\textsuperscript{2,3} (Figure 5A). Adélie penguins disperse
east towards the Weddell Sea but by contrast, Gentoo penguins from Cape
Shirreff used mainly APBSW during winter\textsuperscript{8} (Figure 4C).

Re-estimating expected performance using corrected LKB and LHR values
demonstrated that penguin performance is insensitive to both metrics.
Differences in ONI conditions remain the primary driver of performance
(Figure 6B). These results contradict the findings of Watters et al.
(2020). High LHR (≥ 0.10) cases showed better performance than the
``best case'' scenario. Data scarcity in high LHR cases, except for
specific winter points, limits these results (Supplementary Material,
Fig. S8). Allocating March data to winter season reveals that
intermediate harvest cases (0.01 \textless{} LHR 0.10) show poorer
performance than originally reported (Fig. 7A-C). Restricting foraging
to specific SSMUs produces expected performance levels for the ``worst
case'' scenario at or above the long-term mean. Specifically, case 5
(ONI 78 ≤ -0.5°C; LKB ≤ 1 Mt; and LHR ≥ 0.1) is actually better than the
`best case' identified by Watters et al.~(2020), while the `worst case'
is at or above the average penguin performance (Fig. 7).

Incorporating known ecological migration patterns reduces the
probability of negative impacts from high LHR, dropping from 93\% to
21\%-37\% (POINT TO RESULTS), or from a near-certainty to approximately
1-in-3 or 1-in-5 (Tables 2-3; Fig. 7). At this point, we clarify that
the probability of negative impact measures the likelihood of an
occurrence rather than the scale of population decline. Given the
temporal separation between fishing and penguin breeding over the
preceding decades, our results are unsurprising.

\subsubsection{Issue \#3: Omission of competing
species}\label{issue-3-omission-of-competing-species-1}

A mean 9,653 (+/- 6346, range 2,429 -- 14,333) humpback whales were
estimated to be present in gSSMU \#1, resulting in a consumption
estimate of 549,088 tonnes across a 90 day period. Summer fishery
removals for the period 1982-2016 summed to 435,493 tonnes for gSSMU
\#1.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

Our reanalysis of~Watters et al.~(2020)~reveals that the study's
conclusions are based on~assumptions that lack support. By
addressing~spatial and temporal mismatches,~unvalidated ecological
assumptions, and~methodological biases, we demonstrate that
the~perceived impacts of krill fishing on penguin performance are
significantly overestimated, reducing the estimated probability of
negative impacts from high LHR~(≥ 0.1) from 93\% to 37\%.

\subsubsection{Issue \#1: Unvalidated ecological
assumptions}\label{issue-1-unvalidated-ecological-assumptions}

A key component of Watters et al.~(2020) analysis is the imputation of
missing summer survey data (LKB), which in turns affect the presumptive
fishing impact (LHR). In addition, they imputed specific range values
for LKB and LHR as `best' and `worst' case scenarios, without testing
their assumptions. Our results indicated that the assumed relationship
of LKB with SAM index sign, and the use of LKB = 1 Mt for assigning good
and bad years are incorrect (Table 1, Figure 2). Furthermore, the
process to estimate imputed-LKB values largely overestimate the
imputed-LKB. When considered altogether, these errors introduce strong
biases in the analysis.

Conclusions on fishing impact on penguins performance were driven by
winter LKB estimates (Figure S7 taken from 9), evident in Figure 3 where
only the winter LHR for 2015 is \textgreater0.10. However in Watters et
al.~2020 winter data were not imputed due to the low number of samples.
After filtering for poor coverage, only 2 winter LKB were retained at
each gSSMU for the entire study (Figure 3) which is in agreement with
dataset descriptions10. The large LHR value for 2015 comes from a low
LKB estimate of 176,394 tonnes, coupled with an increase in winter
catches (Figure 3).

Watters et al.~(2020) assumes a direct relationship between~krill
biomass and the phase of the Southern Annular Mode (SAM), yet our
statistical analyses reveal~no significant correlation~between SAM
phases and Local Krill Biomass (LKB). Similarly, the assumption that~low
LKB (\textless1 Mt) corresponds to smaller krill~is not supported by
empirical evidence. This raises concerns over the extrapolation of the
ecological significance of small versus large krill in the context of
penguin performance used in Watters et al.~2020.

\subsubsection{Issue \#2: Spatial and temporal mismatches undermine
study
conclusions}\label{issue-2-spatial-and-temporal-mismatches-undermine-study-conclusions}

The~spatial scales~used in Watters et al.~(2020) exceed the actual
foraging ranges of penguins by a factor of~4 to 15. For example, Adélie
penguins forage within~1,139 nm², yet the study used~gSSMUs of
15,500--20,600 nm²~to define ``local'' krill biomass and harvest rates.
This mismatch leads to~overestimated localized depletion effects, as the
broad spatial units fail to capture the fine-scale interactions between
penguins and their prey.

Similarly, the study's~temporal assumptions~incorrectly associate winter
fishing pressure with penguin performance, despite Adélie and chinstrap
penguins~migrating away from breeding colonies by late February. By
excluding these species from winter analyses, we show that the~original
model's conclusions about seasonal fishing impacts are misassigned.

\subsubsection{Issue \#3: Omission of competing krill
predators}\label{issue-3-omission-of-competing-krill-predators}

A major limitation of Watters et al.~(2020) is the omission of
krill-dependent predators that compete with penguins for prey resources.
Recovering baleen whale populations exert substantial predation pressure
on krill, particularly in regions that overlap with penguin breeding and
foraging activity. Using available abundance, distribution and
consumption estimates for competing species, we show that humpback
whales in one summer season consumed as much krill in gSSMU \#1 as taken
by the fishery during the entire 32-year period of the Watters et al.
(2020) study. The consumption by whales also overlaps temporally with
penguin breeding, unlike the fishery which since 2010 has shifted to a
winter fishery along the Peninsula5. Intuitively then, ignoring the vast
amount more krill removed by whales at the same time as breeding
penguins are foraging is likely to be greatly overestimating the
relative impact of fisheries removal on penguin performance. Future
assessments of krill fishery must incorporate~multi-species
interactions~to accurately evaluate the cumulative pressures on
krill-dependent predators. It is worth noting that our evaluation of
competing predators is limited to whales; scientific evidence suggests
that seal and fish predation on krill is also nontrivial11,12.

\subsubsection{Broader considerations}\label{broader-considerations}

While Watters et al.~(2020) raises important questions regarding the
ecological impacts of krill fishery, an analytical framework~focussing
solely on fishing pressure and climate indices is~insufficient~for
disentangling the~relative contributions of multiple stressors affecting
krill-dependent predators. Over~20 years ago, CCAMLR acknowledged that
its~CEMP monitoring program~was unable to distinguish between the
effects of fishing and climate variability13, yet no substantive
modifications have been implemented to address this limitation. In this
context, a further limitation of Watters et al.~(2020) is its reliance
on macroscale climate indices, such as the Oceanic Niño Index (ONI) and
the Southern Annular Mode (SAM), to explain variability in krill
availability and penguin performance. While these indices are useful for
characterizing large-scale atmospheric and oceanographic patterns, their
application to ecological processes at fine spatial and temporal scales
relevant to penguin foraging is problematic. The Amundsen Sea Low (ASL)
is the dominant climate feature for the western Antarctic
Peninsula14--16. The El Niño-Southern Oscillation (captured in the ONI
predictor) modulates the ASL, with El Niño (La Niña) shallowing
(deepening) its pressure, causing more northwesterly (southeasterly)
winds and upwelling (restricted influx) of Circumpolar Deep Water onto
the shelf. The SAM also influences the pressure of the ASL, with the
current trend of negative SAM constructively (destructively) interfering
with ASL when in phase with El Niño (La Niña) events17. The result is a
set of above-surface climate conditions that drive changes in water mass
intrusion that is in turn dependent on interactions between two climate
processes as well as the geographical orientation of the coastline
involved, all of which have been shown to dramatically impact the
foraging trajectories and trip durations of Chinstrap penguins18. The
bathymetry of the Antarctic Peninsula, which also influences the
hydrographic conditions, is complex particularly at scales that are
important to centrally-foraging predators such as penguins. The
structuring of krill aggregations in time and space in the WAP have also
been linked to mesoscale circulation processes19, which are unlikely to
be uniformly affected by broad-scale climate indices due to local
variation in bathymetry and coastal orientation.

The~real-world implications~of Watters et al.~(2020) are substantial, as
its conclusions have influenced~CCAMLR's Marine Protected Area
proposals~and~the Marine Stewardship Council fisheries certification
processes. Our reanalyses suggest that key~methodological limitations,
including spatial and temporal mismatches, unvalidated and often
scientifically unjustified ecological assumptions, and the omission of
competing predators compromise the study`s validity. We recommend future
assessments: 1. Use~spatial scales aligned with penguin foraging
ranges~such as SSMUs rather than gSSMUs to better reflect ecological
interaction, though we caution that even the SSMU scale is likely
insufficient to capture these ecological interactions in a meaningful
way. 2. Account for~species-specific migration and seasonal variability
in fishing effort, particularly in relation to breeding phenology. 3.
Incorporate~competing predators~and~local environmental drivers,
including mesoscale oceanographic processes and krill life-stage
dynamics. By addressing these issues, managers can develop~more accurate
and evidence-based strategies~for balancing krill harvesting with the
conservation of dependent predator populations.

\subsubsection{Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

Our reanalysis of Watters et al.~(2020) demonstrates that the study's
conclusions are substantially influenced by methodological limitations,
including spatial and spatial/temporal mismatches, unsupported
ecological assumptions, the omission of a key competing krill predator
and structural biases in model design. While~climate variability
(ONI)~remains a dominant driver of penguin performance, the~impacts of
krill fishing are considerably less severe~when evaluated at~more
ecologically relevant spatial and temporal scales.

To ensure that scientific advice provided to CCAMLR and other management
and certification agencies is both objective and robust, future
assessments must adopt~finer resolution spatial frameworks aligned with
predator foraging ranges, account for species-specific seasonal
dynamics, and incorporate~multi-species interactions and local
environmental drivers. Only through such integrative approaches can we
accurately assess the cumulative impacts of fishing and environmental
change~on Antarctic krill-dependent predators in the Southern ocean
ecosystem.
